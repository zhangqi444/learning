# ISEE Lower Level — the item spec every question, practice set and mock is written to

Written 4 October 2026, after the owner asked what guidance the site's questions
had been generated from and to make sure it was the right one. Until then there was
none in the repo beyond the section counts and AGENTS.md's general content rules
(no invented facts, correct keys, written for a ten-year-old). The banks and the
four mock forms were authored to a general idea of the test, and
`docs/mock-review-2026-10-04.md` shows where that drifted.

## Sources, in order of authority

1. **ERB, *What to Expect on the ISEE — Lower Level***, the guide ERB itself
   describes as "the only authorized guide produced by ERB", written by the people
   who write the test. The edition read here is July 2016 (144 pages: section
   descriptions, sample questions by strand, a full practice test with answer key
   and conversion tables). The Lower Level blueprint has not changed since — the
   same 34/38/25/30 and a 30-minute essay. The owner should get the current
   edition from ERB; where it differs from this file, it wins and this file is
   updated.
2. **The Princeton Review, *Lower Level ISEE Practice Test*** (© 2024), the paper
   the owner put in Drive on 4 October and the one she sits on paper
   (`/mock/TPR`). A cross-check, not an authority: where it departs from ERB (it
   uses exponents and prime factorizations written with exponents, which ERB's
   samples do not), ERB wins.
3. **Never memory.** A rule here cites one of the two, or it is not a rule.

Neither book's questions are copied into this repo, here or anywhere: both are
copyrighted and the repo is public. This file records their *specifications* —
counts, shares, conventions, scope — which is what an item writer needs.

The real test carries a few unscored pilot items in each section, which is why
ERB's own practice test is shorter (30/35/20/25); the site's mocks keep the full
counts and times, which is right.

Every multiple-choice item has four choices, A–D.

## Answer-choice order — the official convention

ERB states it outright: one-word choices are listed **alphabetically**, phrase
choices are **ordered by length**, and numbers run **in ascending order** (both
references follow this on every item). ERB even teaches it as a strategy for
synonyms — think of the word, then find it in the alphabetical list.

So the key's letter falls wherever the content puts it. Items are not shuffled to
balance A–D: a content-determined order cannot carry a generator's pattern, which
was the reason the banks were re-shuffled in August, and it is the format she
will meet on the day.

## Verbal Reasoning

- **Synonyms (Part One, about 17).** A headword "abstract, grade-level
  appropriate", four single-word choices of the same part of speech, alphabetical.
  **Mix the parts of speech**: ERB's practice test has 6 verbs, 4 adjectives, 3
  nouns and 2 adverbs in 15; the Princeton Review paper 8 nouns, 4 verbs, 4
  adjectives and 1 adverb in 17. No part of speech above half a form.
- **Sentence completion (Part Two, about 17).** One blank; the answer is "a word
  or phrase". **About a quarter to a third are phrase completions** (ERB 5 of
  15, Princeton Review 4 of 17), placed at the end of Part Two, where the whole
  phrase is the logical conclusion of the sentence. Clue types: contrast is
  emphasized (*although, but, unlike, rather*), then cause (*because, since,
  so*), definition and example. Contexts are cross-curricular — science, history,
  the arts, real people — as well as everyday life.

## Quantitative Reasoning

- Lower Level is **word problems only** — no quantitative comparisons (those are
  Middle and Upper). ERB: problems "require little or no calculation; the
  emphasis is on your ability to reason mathematically".
- **About half the items work from something drawn**: a figure, diagram, table,
  graph, number line or Venn diagram (ERB's practice test about 18 of 35; the
  Princeton Review paper about 14 of 38). Reading the picture is part of the skill.
- **In scope** (ERB's strands): numbers and operations; algebra — patterns,
  input/output rules, one- and two-step equations, words into an equation;
  geometry — shapes, symmetry and folding, perimeter, counting cubes; measurement
  — units, map scales; data and probability — reading graphs and tables, mean
  and range, the probability of a *simple* event, as "x out of y".
- **Out of scope** (in neither reference): negative numbers; percent change,
  discounts and reverse percents; compound probability (two draws, without
  replacement); dividing a fraction by a fraction; evaluating expressions with
  exponents; how area or volume scales when every dimension is multiplied.

## Reading Comprehension

- Five passages of five questions each. **Length about 190–340 words** (ERB's four
  practice passages run about 190 to 335; the Princeton Review's five about 170 to
  370). A form's passages stay inside that band so one form is not easier because
  it is shorter.
- Subjects, per ERB: **arts, contemporary life, history and science**. Both
  references include **biography of a real historical person** and narrative
  fiction (a folktale in the Princeton Review paper). Real people bring real
  facts, so a biographical passage cites its sources like any other fact on this
  site (AGENTS.md, "Never invent a fact").
- **Passages carry line numbers, and about a third of the questions cite them**
  ("In line 21 …", "the third paragraph (lines 14–24)") — ERB 8 of 20, the
  Princeton Review 9 of 25.
- Question categories, as ERB reports them: main idea; supporting ideas;
  inference; vocabulary in context ("most nearly means", with a line);
  organization/logic (the function of a paragraph, "the passage provides
  information to answer which question?"); tone, style and figurative language
  (author's attitude, what kind of writing the passage resembles). An occasional
  EXCEPT question.

## Mathematics Achievement

- Items "conform to national mathematics standards", one or more steps.
- ERB's strands: whole numbers (place value, standard form, number names,
  operations with regrouping); decimals, percents and fractions (equivalent forms
  — a percent appears as another way to write a fraction or decimal); algebraic
  concepts (the missing number, simple equations, sequences); geometry;
  measurement (perimeter, area, elapsed time, time zones, units); data analysis
  and probability (read graphs, tables and maps; mean, median, mode and range;
  simple-event probability).
- **About 40% use a graph, table, number line, map or figure** (ERB 10 of 25,
  the Princeton Review 11 of 30 — pictographs, bar graphs, price lists).
- The same out-of-scope list as Quantitative Reasoning.

## Essay

One sentence, personal or opinion, in plain words: ERB's practice prompt asks what
one thing she would change about her community and why; the Princeton Review's,
what her perfect vacation would be. Not a multi-part checklist of what the essay
must contain.

## Writing a wrong choice

Every wrong choice is a place a child arrives by a nameable error, and its `why`
names that error: "30 is 10 × 3, the area, not the perimeter." A `why` that only
back-checks ("30 is too big; 2 × 13 is 26") teaches nothing, and half the old
mock items had one. Three further rules from the review:

- **The trap must not give the key.** When the mean equals the median, when the
  largest numerator is also the largest fraction, when the last digit is the
  remainder, the item cannot catch the error it was written for. Change a number.
- **No format cues.** The key is never the only decimal, the only fraction in
  lowest terms, the only choice written in primes, the only phrase among words;
  fractions are always printed in lowest terms; a number in the stem is never the
  key.
- **Reading distractors are anchored in the passage** — a plausible misreading, a
  detail from the wrong paragraph, a true statement that does not answer the
  question. An absolute (*always, never, every, only*) is not filler; a child who
  has learned to strike absolutes should get nothing for it. A passage does not
  end on a stated lesson that the main-idea item can lift.

## Figures and line numbers

An item carries its picture as a small declarative `figure` — table, bar, line,
pictograph, numberline, grid, polygon, venn, spinner, clock, shaded, cubes — whose
schema is `tools/itemspec.py` (`FIGURES`) and whose drawing is
`site/src/components/figure.jsx`. The stem introduces the figure; the figure holds
the data.

A passage written to this spec carries `lines: true` and is authored line by line:
a newline ends a line, a blank line ends a paragraph, lines run to about 56
characters like the booklet column. The page numbers every line from 1
(`NumberedLines` in `site/src/pages/runner.jsx`), and the validator checks that a
stem citing "line 14" quotes a word that is on line 14. Nothing else may appear
in a passage's text: the `[¶n]` and `[Sn]` tags Mock 2 and Mock 3 once carried
printed on her screen until the review caught them.

## Which forms are held to this

`OFFICIAL_FORMS` in `tools/itemspec.py` lists the forms the validator holds to the
official choice order, the passage band and the line numbers. A form is added when
it is rewritten whole, never half-converted: a balanced-letter form was shuffled
on purpose, and a child who has met one shuffled set learns nothing from the rest
being in order. Adding a form turns the gate red until every item of it complies,
which is the point.

## Forms are parallel

A mock is only useful as a measurement if the next one is the same paper with
different questions. Every form follows this spec in the same proportions —
passage lengths in the band, the same part-of-speech mix, phrase completions in
the same place, a comparable spread of headword difficulty — so a change from one
mock to the next is her, not the paper.

## What the site needs to follow it

Two parts of this spec cannot be met by writing alone: drawn figures, graphs and
tables need a renderer in the runner, and line-numbered passages need the passage
view to print line numbers. Until those exist, an item that needs a picture
cannot be written honestly, and the share of such items is the measure of what
the site still cannot ask.
